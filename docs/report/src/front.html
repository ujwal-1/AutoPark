@@ABS@@Abstract

Finding a free parking space in dense commercial and campus environments is slow, wasteful and difficult to monitor. Conventional “smart” parking installations solve the visibility problem by instrumenting every bay with an ultrasonic, magnetic or infrared sensor, which multiplies hardware cost, cabling and maintenance with lot size. This project presents AutoPark, a smart parking system that replaces per-bay sensing with two camera nodes at the entry and exit gates. Each node captures a frame every five seconds, transmits it to a FastAPI backend, and the backend locates the licence plate with a YOLOv11 detector and reads it with EasyOCR using four preprocessing variants and multi-frame voting.

The system supports both guaranteed advance pre-bookings and unreserved walk-in entry: a pre-booked vehicle is routed to its held bay, while a walk-in vehicle is automatically allocated the next available bay, raising the SG90 boom barrier and highlighting the assigned bay on the live dashboard. If the lot is full, or if the evidence for a plate is ambiguous, the barrier remains closed. Exit is handled symmetrically: the plate is matched against active sessions, the bay is released, and an itemised receipt is produced. Because occupancy is derived from entry and exit events rather than measured bay by bay, the sensing hardware is constant in the number of bays instead of growing with it.

The implementation comprises a six-bay facility, a real-time Next.js dashboard, a role-aware operator console, a monochrome operations design system, and a hardware-in-the-loop simulator that exercises the same endpoints as the physical cameras. Verification is reported as it stands rather than as it was hoped to stand: twelve of fifteen end-to-end checks pass, eleven functional behaviours were confirmed directly through the HTTP interface — bay binding, walk-in allocation, duplicate suppression, low-confidence refusal, full-lot refusal, exit release and both tariff boundaries — and the complete fifty-seven-test suite is red only for two identified reasons, a missing OCR dependency and six assertions that predate the walk-in policy. Six defects found during validation were fixed and six remain open, all disclosed. Recognition accuracy and end-to-end latency are not claimed as measured results in this report: the measurement protocol is defined, two representative diagnostic records are reproduced, and the remaining bench tests are listed explicitly as pending. The report concludes that gate-only occupancy inference is workable at the scale of a department lot, and that its costs — dependence on plate-read quality, one recovery path for unreadable exits, and a policy decision about unbooked arrivals — are predictable and manageable.
